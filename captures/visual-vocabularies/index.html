01 / Drypoint

The searching line

A line searches; it does not seal every edge.

Fine scratched contours, intermittent velvety black, local hatching and large unprinted areas.

Could serve: Quiet portraits, essays, intimate chapter openers.

What I see

An attentive face and an excellent range of thin and heavy marks. It is still more fully modelled than the prompt asked. This is a familiar print vocabulary, not yet a signature.

Style/medium: a spare drypoint impression on clean pale ivory rag paper. Everything is made from scratched black lines: fine wiry searching contours, occasional heavy velvety burr at a changed direction, small knots of irregular crosshatching. A line is allowed to stop before an outline closes. No filled flat shapes, no washes, no color; even the orange and blue bowl are represented only in black ink. Let three lightly contradictory marks remain around the cheek and wrist, as observation rather than cartoon wobble. The face should feel specifically observed, middle-aged and tired around the eyes, drawn with very few unequal strokes. The chair and lower torso are mostly omitted. About two thirds of the image remains completely unprinted; the open window is suggested by only two unjoined lines. Intimate close composition with head and working hands offset toward the lower right; the long orange peel supplies the loose visual counterline. Shading is local and sparse, not an all-over field of hatch. The needle, ink and pressure determine the edges. Avoid generalized distressed paper, sepia, perfect vector contours, symmetrical hatching, cute cartoon features, decorative border, photorealistic rendering.

The real process scratches directly into a plate; its raised burr holds ink and gives a softer, richer line. The Met · Drypoint ↗

02 / Pochoir / stencilled gouache

Colour through a hole

Colour shapes have to describe the person without an outline.

Opaque pigment, a small palette, stencil edges, flat adjacent planes instead of shaded volume.

Could serve: Posters, covers, opening slides and bold educational illustrations.

What I see

The cobalt and vermilion have real punch. But the model added a picturesque view and ignored the six-shape limit. Attractive; also the most likely to slip back into tasteful retro illustration.

03 / Wiped monotype

Light taken out

The light is what has been taken away.

A dark ink field, rag-wiped light, lost edges and one small warm accent.

Could serve: Reflective editorial work, narrative covers and emotionally quiet sequences.

What I see

My strongest mood study. The shoulder disappears into the room and the orange holds the attention. The face is too conventionally resolved for the broad wipes requested; this is monotype-inspired rather than a convincing technical specimen.

In a subtractive, or dark-field, monotype, the plate is inked and the image is formed by removing or moving that ink. MoMA · Monotype ↗

05 / Oil pastel / sgraffito

Scratch back to colour

A scratch reveals another colour; it is not decorative grain.

Compressed wax strokes, scraped underlayers, blunt marks and colour relationships that rub against each other.

Could serve: Expressive editorial illustration and forceful campaign imagery.

What I see

There is good energy in the cheek and sleeves. It also over-rendered the room and returned to a handsome figure. The waxy surface reads more strongly than the scratch-through construction. Promising material, weaker obedience.

06 / Carbon-transfer line + one spot colour · Start here

Carbon & citrus

Broken blue lines. One solid orange. Everything else can stay absent.

Pressure gaps, incomplete contours, an oversize hand, clean white and a single opaque accent.

Could serve: Adult learning, human-scale service stories, spot illustrations and a distinctive editorial series.

What I see

My recommendation to develop next. The line and accent can become repeatable rules. It still completes too much of the face and hand; the next pass should simplify the drawing further. This is an invented hybrid, not a named historical movement.

A vocabulary we can keep.

My recommendation is to develop Carbon & citrus first: interrupted blue contours, one solid accent, very little description of the room. It has room to become recognisable without depending on a nostalgic setting.

Tool + mark behaviour + space + colour rule + what to omit.

For example: exhausted carbon transfer; lines that break with pressure; large untouched areas; one orange accent; no enclosed silhouettes. The scene changes. Those decisions stay.

The next useful test is the same grammar applied to a group of people, a practical task and an object-only image. We have not run that consistency test yet. A style earns its place by surviving it.

We can also test brush-and-ink, wood engraving, technical pen, photocopy and stencil hybrids. The point is to build a small set of languages we can direct, then choose one for what a project needs.
